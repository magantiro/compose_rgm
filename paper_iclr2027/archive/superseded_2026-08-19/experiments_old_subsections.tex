Three qualifications belong with \cref{tab:qed} rather than after it.
The comparison against the published $45.1\%$ at $20$ candidates is \emph{not} matched: a different source panel, so the numbers estimate the same quantity on different populations of sources.
The candidate budget differs conservatively, $8$ and $12$ being fewer than $20$, while the per-candidate compute differs the other way --- one returned candidate here is a particle run of $32\times40=1{,}280$ internal molecule evaluations, so the returned-candidate \emph{interface} matches while the compute behind each returned molecule does not.
% source: docs/AMENDMENT_VALIDATION_128.md ("k = 8 is fewer returned candidates than GrIDDD's 20 ..."; "32 particles x 40 steps = 1,280 internal molecule evaluations")
We state the claim as improved task performance at a measured inference-time cost, never as greater efficiency, and never offer the absence of objective-specific retraining as compensation; the inference ledger is $1{,}024$ runs consuming $1{,}167{,}787$ committed transitions, $248$ contacting the goal region and $776$ ending with no surviving particle, returning $2.58$ distinct molecules per run.
% source: docs/VALID128_K8_RESULT.json runs=1024, work_transitions=1167787, contact_runs=248, extinct_runs=776, mean_distinct_returned=2.578125
This establishes that a controller selected on development sources reproduced its behaviour on a panel never looked at --- not that \method{} outperforms the comparator on its own benchmark: the published contract is $800$ official sources at $20$ candidates, untouched until the last \method{} row exists.
\emph{Limitations.} The development panel is consumed and its curve is selection evidence only; the validation panel carries no solvability strata, which is what makes it prospective; and the resampling unit is the source ($n=128$).

\subsection{An exact one-step tilt of the same process finds nothing where future-aware control succeeds}
\label{sec:exp-attribution}

A sharp developmental answer exists for the strongest cheap alternative: the one-step policy tilts the frozen reference law by the \emph{immediate} property value of each successor over the full legal fiber, $\pi(y\mid x)\propto R_\theta(y\mid x)\,\mathrm{QED}(y)$ --- an exact one-step terminal tilt, parameter-free, preregistered before any outcome existed.
% source: docs/GRIDDD_JIN_PROTOCOL.md, "THE INFERENCE POLICY" (an *exact* one-step terminal tilt)
On the disjoint $64$-source development panel it produced $0$ successes in $320$ trajectories
% source: docs/GRIDDD_JIN_PROTOCOL.md, CURRENT AMENDMENT ("Policy B was refuted 0/320 on the disjoint 64-source development panel"); 320 = 64 sources x 5 replicates, same document
for the expected reason: the target region is typically not reachable in one edit from a source at the low end of the property range, so what a one-step tilt optimizes is nearly uninformative about reachability within the remaining budget.

That is evidence for the necessity of a forward-looking value, not the matched ablation: the arms differ in more than horizon, since the one-step arm reads the true objective of each successor while the deployed controller reads a learned value, and attribution needs both arms to share the landscape exactly --- the design of \eqref{eq:ablation}.
With $\widehat F_\psi$ a property predictor trained on a fixed oracle-label budget and $g_z$ the terminal desirability built from it, the matched arms are
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{\;R_\theta(y\mid x)\;g_z(y)\;}_{\text{immediate}}
\qquad\text{versus}\qquad
\underbrace{\;R_\theta(y\mid x)\;h_\phi(y,z,b-1)\;}_{\text{future-aware}},
\label{eq:ablation}
\end{equation}
run with the same frozen $R_\theta$, legal fibers, $\widehat F_\psi$, $g_z$, sources, seeds and returned-candidate budget.

\subsection{Designed but not measured: multi-objective control and process-level capabilities}
\label{sec:exp-multiobj}
\label{sec:exp-capabilities}

The second benchmark asks whether the construction transfers to simultaneous multi-objective optimization; its published protocol includes task-specific training, which is what makes a matched-supervision comparison possible.
It fixes $10{,}000$ oracle labels for training plus $5{,}000$ for optimization on two objectives ($20{,}000$ plus $5{,}000$ on four), five preference vectors and ten molecules per generation, so both methods receive identical supervision and diverge only in what they build from it: the comparator follows the gradient of a fitted property predictor, while \method{} fits the same predictor, scores oracle-free rollouts of the frozen $R_\theta$ with it, and learns the finite-horizon value \eqref{eq:cheby}.
