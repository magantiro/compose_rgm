\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:gem-experiments}

We evaluate COMPOSE at three levels. First, we ask whether the frozen reference
law learns which executable edits are appropriate for a given molecule. Second,
we test whether a molecular process learned once on GuacaMol can be transferred
unchanged to externally defined molecular-design tasks. Finally, we study two
capabilities that standard endpoint benchmarks do not measure: reasoning about
the future consequences of an edit and reusing molecular progress after the
design objective changes.

\subsection{The reference process learns state-dependent edit preferences}
\label{sec:gem-exp-rtheta}

The rewrite system determines which molecular edits are possible. It does not
determine which of those edits should be likely in a particular molecular
state. We therefore ask whether the frozen reference law $R_\theta$ learns
preferences beyond corpus-wide edit-family frequencies.

We evaluate 3{,}545 held-out transitions whose source molecules are disjoint
from reference-law training. The main baseline uses the empirical frequency of
each edit family in the training corpus while retaining the same executable
support and the same canonical molecular successors. The comparison therefore
changes only how probability is distributed among legal next molecules.

$R_\theta$ reduces canonical-successor NLL from 5.37 to 3.90 nats relative to
the empirical-family baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}a). Most of this gain
is state specific. Keeping empirical family frequencies but learning only which
successor to prefer within the current state recovers 86.7\% of the improvement
from 5.37 to 3.90 nats.

These results separate two roles in COMPOSE. The executor defines \textbf{what
is possible}. The learned reference law assigns state-dependent preference to
\textbf{what is plausible next}. We keep $R_\theta$ fixed in all subsequent
experiments. Its probabilities describe the learned molecular design process; we
do not interpret them as physical kinetics or synthetic-route likelihoods.
Results restricted to edit families with data-backed transition supervision are
reported in Appendix~S.1.

\subsection{A molecular process learned once transfers to external design tasks}
\label{sec:gem-exp-external}

We next ask whether the goal-independent molecular process must be retrained for
each downstream design problem. It does not. $R_\theta$ is trained once on
GuacaMol and then frozen. We transfer this same reference process unchanged to
similarity-constrained editing on ZINC-250k and to constrained lead
optimization. Only task-specific control changes.

This separation is deliberate. $R_\theta$ represents reusable molecular
transition structure; the downstream objective determines which parts of that
process are useful for a particular task.

\paragraph{Similarity-constrained QED editing.}
We use the ZINC-250k QED editing benchmark reported by GrIDDD
\citep{ninniri2025griddd}. Source molecules begin with QED in $[0.7,0.8]$. A
source is solved if at least one returned molecule reaches QED $\geq 0.9$ while
retaining Morgan-fingerprint Tanimoto similarity $\geq 0.4$ to the source.

This experiment is also a cross-corpus transfer test of the reference process.
COMPOSE does \textbf{not} retrain $R_\theta$ on ZINC-250k. The GuacaMol-trained
molecular process is carried into the benchmark unchanged, and only the QED
control objective is adapted. By contrast, GrIDDD is trained directly on the
ZINC-250k training split used for its benchmark.

Each COMPOSE candidate is one controlled molecular trajectory. A trajectory
terminates when it first enters the target region; trajectories that never enter
the region remain failures. We do not retrospectively collect intermediate
molecules as additional candidates.

On the current 128-source held-out panel, COMPOSE solves 70/128 sources
(54.7\%) with at most 12 returned candidates per source. The mean within-source
pairwise Tanimoto distance is 0.393, indicating that successful editing does not
collapse to a single narrow family of outputs. GrIDDD reports 45.1\% success
with $K=20$ on the official 800-source benchmark.

Because these results use different source cohorts and candidate budgets, the
128-source result is benchmark context rather than a direct numerical ranking.
The final comparison uses the official 800-source protocol with the frozen
GuacaMol-trained $R_\theta$:
\[
  \boxed{\text{GuacaMol training}}
  \;\rightarrow\;
  \boxed{\text{freeze } R_\theta}
  \;\rightarrow\;
  \boxed{\text{ZINC-250k control}}
\]

The benchmark therefore tests more than QED optimization alone. It asks whether
molecular transition structure learned on one broad chemical corpus can support
controlled editing on another without retraining the molecular reference
process.

\paragraph{Similarity-constrained lead optimization.}
We also evaluate the same frozen process on the lead-optimization protocol
introduced by GenMol \citep{lee2025genmol}. The benchmark contains five protein
targets, three seed molecules per target, and similarity thresholds
$\delta\in\{0.4,0.6\}$.

A returned molecule must satisfy
\[
  \mathrm{QED}\geq 0.6,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{SA}\leq 4,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Tanimoto}(x,x_0)\geq \delta .
\]
Among feasible molecules, performance is measured by QuickVina2 docking score;
lower is better. These are endpoint constraints. Intermediate molecular states
need not satisfy them.

The reference process and executable chemistry are identical to those used
above. COMPOSE changes only the task-specific control signal and uses docking
feedback to search the same molecular process for feasible, high-affinity
endpoints.

The final frozen evaluation uses \textbf{100 docking-oracle calls per cell},
compared with 1{,}000 calls in the published GenMol protocol. We report
feasibility across the 30 benchmark cells, paired docking performance, and
run-to-run variation.

The separation between inexpensive constraints and the expensive docking
objective is useful in this setting. QED, SA, similarity, and chemical validity
are evaluated before docking. The expensive oracle is therefore spent only on
candidate molecules that satisfy the benchmark constraints.

Together, these benchmarks establish external competence of the frozen molecular
process. They also test two distinct forms of reuse: transfer across chemical
corpora in the ZINC experiment, and transfer across downstream objectives in
constrained lead optimization. Neither benchmark, however, asks what can be done
with the molecular trajectory itself. We study that next.

\subsection{Capabilities beyond fixed-objective endpoint optimization}
\label{sec:gem-exp-lookahead}

Standard molecular-design benchmarks evaluate returned molecules under a fixed
objective. They do not test two consequences of representing design as a process
over complete molecular states. First, the quality of an edit can depend on the
molecular futures it leaves reachable. Second, a molecule reached during
optimization can remain useful if the objective later changes.

We construct controlled evaluations to isolate these two capabilities.

\paragraph{Future consequences can change the best molecular edit.}
We first ask whether a locally attractive edit can become a poor choice once its
downstream consequences are considered.

We use 65 held-out source--target pairs. Every pair has a certified executable
path to the target within the available edit budget. The local and future-aware
policies use the same source, target, frozen $R_\theta$, executable successors,
and edit budget.

The local policy chooses the successor with highest immediate target similarity.
The future-aware policy evaluates what remains reachable over the remaining
budget. It overrides the local decision only when another successor has a
strictly better continuation.

The difference is substantial. Local selection reaches 26/65 targets.
Remaining-budget lookahead reaches 40/65, rescuing 14 of the 39 local failures:
\[
  26/65 \rightarrow 40/65,
  \qquad
  +21.5 \text{ percentage points } [12.3,\,33.5].
\]

Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}b shows one deterministic rescue. The local policy
chooses the successor with higher immediate similarity, $0.737$, but fails to
reach the target. The future-aware policy instead chooses a lower-similarity
successor, $0.718$, because that state retains a successful continuation. The
target is then reached within budget.

Across the full panel, the conclusion is simple: \textbf{the value of a
molecular edit can depend on what remains possible after that edit, not only on
the molecule it produces immediately.}

Exhaustively evaluating every continuation is expensive. We therefore also use
$h_\phi$ to prioritize which successors receive explicit rollout evaluation.
This preserves the 40/65 recovery rate while using 34.6\% of the continuation
evaluations required by all-candidate lookahead
(Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}d). We treat this as an efficiency diagnostic rather
than as a separate benchmark claim; full details are in Appendix~S.2.

\paragraph{Realized molecular progress can be reused after an objective switch.}
We next ask whether molecular progress remains useful when the design objective
changes after optimization has already begun.

Starting from $x_0$, COMPOSE executes three edits under an initial objective and
reaches a realized molecular state $x_3$. We then change the objective. The
comparison keeps the new objective and remaining edit budget fixed:
\[
  \text{continue from } x_3
  \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad
  \text{restart from } x_0 .
\]
The experiment therefore asks whether progress already made in molecular space
can be reused, rather than whether the new task can eventually be solved from
scratch.

Across 40 held-out molecules, continuing from the realized state outperforms
restarting in both switch directions. Under the potency-first switch, the paired
gain in post-switch utility is
\[
  +0.302\;[0.186,\,0.413].
\]
Under the developability-first switch, the gain is
\[
  +0.176\;[0.069,\,0.283].
\]
The continued trajectories also move strongly toward the new objective rather
than simply preserving the old one.

This result isolates a second consequence of the process representation. The
state reached during optimization is not discarded when the goal changes. It
remains a complete molecule inside the same executable state space and can
immediately become the starting point for new control.

Future-aware control and retargeting therefore test two complementary forms of
intervention. Future-aware control changes a decision because of \textbf{what
may happen later}. Retargeting changes what happens next because \textbf{the
objective itself has changed}. Both are natural operations on a reusable
molecular process, but neither is captured by standard fixed-objective endpoint
benchmarks.
